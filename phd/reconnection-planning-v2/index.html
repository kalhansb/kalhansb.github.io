Reconnection Planning V2

How the robots share out the forest and sweep it cell by cell

V2 keeps V1’s radios and map swapping, and changes how each robot explores. The forest is cut into 25 cells of 20 m, and a robot sweeps a cell along a short route of viewpoints instead of driving to one goal at a time. In two of the four arms, the robots also stop sharing out the cells as they go, and follow lists made before the run. In every arm here, robots swap maps whenever their radios reach each other, and no robot chases a teammate or sets up a meeting. The scenes below are small toys. Measured results follow them, and every number the rules use is listed at the end.

How one robot sweeps a cell

The map is the same as in V1: ground not seen yet, free ground, and blocked ground (a trunk plus a 1.5 m safety ring). The forest is a 100 m square cut into 5 × 5 cells of 20 m, and the centre cell is home. Every 5 s each robot checks how much of each cell is still unknown on its own map. A cell is finished at 10% unknown or less. Arms A, PH and PC sweep a cell as shown here. Arm R still drives to one goal at a time, as in V1.

    The six cells, in list order

      Keeping the route up to date

      • After passing a viewpoint, the robot plans the route again from where it is. It takes the new route only if the first viewpoint moved more than 1.25 m, or the length changed by more than 10%.
      • A viewpoint within 1.25 m of one on the old route keeps its old place in the order. A fresh order wins only if it is at least 10% shorter.
      • It drops any viewpoint that turns out to be blocked. Once a second, it also drops the next one if less than 20 m² of what it was picked to see is still unknown. After a drop it plans again, or ends the route if nothing is left.

      When a route ends

      • As soon as its cell is finished, even with viewpoints left. The robot plans the next cell at once, without stopping.
      • At the last viewpoint, the robot stops within 0.4 m and waits 2 s for its map to catch up, as after a goal in V1.
      • If no spot sees 20 m² of the cell or the next one, the robot uses V1’s step for once and drives to a single goal.

      Getting stuck

      • The route fails if the robot moves less than 0.2 m in 15 s or runs over its time limit. The next viewpoint goes on the failed list.
      • Time limit: each leg gets its length at 0.15 m/s, times 3, kept between 30 s and 180 s. The length is the straight line to the viewpoint, or for the first leg the planned path if that is longer. The clock restarts whenever the next viewpoint changes.
      • If the robot stops to give way to a teammate (Part C), it sends the rest of the route again when it drives on.

      The real runs behave the same way. Across the A, PH and PC runs, 8 in 10 routes had one or two viewpoints, and none had more than five. For each viewpoint a robot drove past, it dropped about four, because it had already seen their ground on the way.

      Finishing the run

      • A robot is finished once every cell is at most 10% unknown on its own map. It checks this every 5 s and whenever it plans, and the first time is enough. It then drives home (Part C).
      • Every robot waits 60 s at the start. A robot also counts as finished after 300 s of finding nothing to plan without moving 1 m, or after 500 planning steps.

      Writing a cell off

      • Each time the robot plans for the cell it is working, and the cell’s unknown hasn’t fallen since the last time, that is a strike. Any fall clears the strikes. In A, PH and PC only a route counts, and a single goal neither adds nor clears one. In R every goal counts.
      • After 3 strikes the robot checks the cell once more. If it still isn’t finished, it is written off for the whole team: no robot works it again, and the finish rule skips it. Home is never written off, and neither is a cell no robot has seen into yet.

      R and A: share out the cells as you go

      V1’s rule on bigger cells. Each robot keeps its own copy of the board of 25 cells, showing which are finished, and sends it in its beacon. From its copy, each robot shares out the unfinished cells as one tour per robot, so that the last robot finishes as early as possible, and works the first cell of its own tour (R takes goals in or next to that cell first). When the link drops, the copies drift apart and some cells get done twice. In this toy, a robot does a cell by reaching its middle.

        Which cells a route uses

        • The route serves the first cell on the robot’s tour, or the second if no spot sees 20 m² of the first.
        • Viewpoints may stand only in cells on the robot’s tour.

        When the tour is empty

        • Every cell is finished as far as the robot’s board shows, counting what teammates told it, but its own map can still lack some of that ground.
        • It then fills holes anywhere on its own map, one goal at a time, until its own map passes the finish rule or a teammate’s map arrives.
        The real lists

        PH and PC: lists made before the run

        PH and PC share nothing out during the run. Before it, the 24 outer cells are split into one list per robot, and each robot works its own list in order. These are the lists the runs used.

        Start of a list Numbers: the order of the cells Where the robot goes to help
        How it works
        1. Every list has the same number of cells: 12, 8 or 6. It starts next to home, moves each time to a neighbouring cell, and ends at home.
        2. The robot works the first cell on its list that isn’t finished. Cells a teammate has finished are skipped. The order never changes.
        3. When its own list is done, the robot helps one set teammate. It goes to that teammate’s last cell before home and works the list backwards, so the two meet partway.
        4. Then it drives home and waits within 1 m. It stops planning but keeps swapping maps, and is finished once every cell is 90% known on its own map. If a cell turns unfinished again, it goes back to work.

        How PH’s lists were picked

        • A time model scores every possible split: 51.2 s to cross a cell, 1 s plus 0.4 rad/s for each turn, and 2.56 s per metre for the trip home.
        • PH takes the split with the earliest finish for the last robot: 713, 576 and 397 s in the model for 2, 3 and 4 robots. The search tries every split.

        How PC’s lists were picked

        • The same model, plus contact: how much of the time the robots would be in radio range on those lists, counting late in the run more.
        • Among splits that finish within 25% of the fastest, PC takes the best trade, where 1 point of contact is worth 4.2 s with 2 robots and 7.1 s with 4. Its lists take 751 s and 429 s in the model.

        No PC with 3 robots

        • Of all 216 equal splits for 3 robots, PH’s lists already had the most contact, so there was nothing to change.
        C

        How robots talk to each other

        Exactly as in V1, and the same in every arm. In V2 this is the whole team layer: robots swap maps when their radios reach, and nobody does anything to get back in range.

        Toy scene

        Hearing each other and swapping maps

        Every robot sends a short beacon once a second. Trunks block the radio. When two robots are in contact, they swap maps until each has everything the other had.

        0 s

          What a beacon carries

          • Where the robot is, and where it is going next.
          • Which teammates it hears directly.
          • Whether it has finished, whether it knows the whole team has finished, and whether it is home.
          • How much map it has sent, and how much it has received from each teammate.
          • Its copy of the board: which cells are finished or written off, as far as it knows.

          Words the rules use

          • Present: its beacon arrived straight from it in the last 10 s. Beacons are never passed on, so hearing about a teammate from someone else doesn’t count.
          • In contact: I hear you, and your beacon says you hear me.
          • All in contact: every pair in the team is in contact, as far as the beacons show.

          Swapping maps, and finishing together

          • A swap is done once each robot has received everything the other had sent when the contact began. It gives up after 120 s with no new data, or 600 s in total.
          • A robot sends only its own map. To get a teammate’s map, it has to be in contact with that teammate itself.
          • A finished robot knows the whole team has finished once it hears every teammate say it has finished too, or hears any beacon that says the whole team has finished.

          Every arm

          Driving home, and giving way

          Exploring drives a route (or a goal, in R). The trip home is a plain drive to a point. These rules hold in every arm.

          Driving home

          • The drive home after finishing ends within 1 m of home. In PH and PC, the drive home to wait also ends within 1 m.
          • If the robot moves less than 0.2 m in 15 s on the way home, it makes a short escape move of 1.5–6 m, then carries on.
          • The drive home gives up after 600 s, and the robot stops where it is.

          Giving way

          • Robots give way in a fixed order: R2 gives way to R1, R3 to R1 and R2, and so on.
          • A driving robot, exploring included, stops when a teammate it gives way to is moving within 1.5 m of it.
          • It drives on once that teammate is 2.5 m away, or has stood still for 10 s at least 1.5 m away, or after 120 s at most. After a 120 s stop, the same teammate can’t stop it again for 30 s unless it moves.

          Measured in the full simulator, not in the toys: 30 runs for each arm with 2, 3 and 4 robots (PC with 2 and 4 only). “Finished” is when the last robot’s map passed the finish rule. These times are not comparable with V1’s, because V2’s finish rule is stricter.

          The last robot finished 18%, 11% and 12% sooner with 2, 3 and 4 robots, and each robot drove 15–18% less.

          Stored lists: PH against A

          25%, 28% and 33% sooner, driving 24–36% less. PH was also the steadiest arm. With 2 robots its runs spread over 678–790 s, against 667–1421 s for A.

          Finish: 732, 682 and 663 sA: 982, 941 and 991 s

          Where PH’s lead comes from

          Part of it is covering the cells sooner. The team had every cell at 659, 482 and 414 s in PH, against 835, 590 and 516 s in A.

          The rest is the wait for maps. With 3 or 4 robots, R and A spent 29–48% of the run (308–474 s) after the team had every cell, while the maps were still reaching the last robot. In PH the robots meet at home, and that wait was 200 s and 249 s.

          Share of the lead from covering sooner: 70%, 42%, 31%with 2, 3 and 4 robots

          Radio contact: PH against A

          Fixed lists change how often robots are in range. With 2 robots, PH splits the forest into west and east, and pairs were in contact 23.7% of the time against 28.9% in A. With 4 robots it went the other way: 43.0% against 37.0%. With 3, about the same (36.2% against 34.6%).

          Time cut off in gaps of 2 minutes or more, per robot: 565 s in A and 532 s in PH with 2 robots, 271 and 286 s with 3, 163 and 164 s with 4. As a share of the run, PH looks worse with 2 robots (73% against 55%), only because its runs are shorter.

          Longest gap between two robots: 421 → 532 s, 547 → 409 s, 515 → 367 sA → PH, with 2, 3 and 4 robots

          Contact-aware lists: PC against PH

          With 2 robots, PC’s north–south split kept pairs in contact 27.8% of the time against 23.7%, cut the time cut off in long gaps from 532 to 333 s per robot, and shortened the longest gap from 532 to 314 s. With 4 robots: contact 45.4% against 43.0%, long gaps 164 → 103 s per robot.

          Fewer PC robots had to wait at home: 22 of 60 against 48 of 60 with 2 robots.

          Finish: 674 against 732 s (8% sooner), 637 against 663 s (4%)with 2 and 4 robots

          PC’s robots finish farther from home. Counting the drive home, PC took 823 s against PH’s 814 s with 2 robots (no clear difference), and 673 against 699 s with 4.

          A second forest

          The same tests on a patchy forest: the same 257 trees per hectare, most of them gathered into 19 thickets. PH beat A again with every team size: 16%, 22% and 34% sooner (998 → 841 s, 771 → 605 s, 932 → 618 s). PC beat PH with 2 robots, 18% sooner (841 → 690 s), but not with 4 (618 → 601 s, no clear difference).

          With 2 robots, PH’s patchy runs spread over 717–1240 s. In 6 of its 30 runs, the helping robot redid 3 or more cells its teammate had already finished, because it hadn’t heard they were finished.

          A difference counts as clear when it holds up after correcting for the many comparisons made at once. In the main forest, all six planned tests (A against R and PH against A, with each team size) and all four PC tests are clear. No run was cut off unfinished.
